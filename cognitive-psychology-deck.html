The Stroop Effect (Stroop, 1935) appears when the written word and the ink colour disagree. Naming the colour becomes slower and more error-prone because reading is automatic: the word's meaning activates even when we try to ignore it.

Selective attention: focusing on one feature (the ink colour) while filtering out another (the word).
Automatic processing: reading is so practised that it happens without any intention.
Cognitive interference: the conflicting word meaning competes with the colour response and slows it down.
Discussion Question

Slide 3 • Timeline 1 of 3

Early Roots & the Behaviorist Era

Late 1800s – 1950s

From introspection to observable behaviour

Late 1800s

Early Experimental Psychology

  • Studied the mind through introspection.
  • Included Wundt's structuralism and James's functionalism.
1920s–1950s

Behaviorist Dominance

  • Led by Watson and Skinner, psychology shifted strictly to observable behaviour.
  • The model became S → R (stimulus to response).
Behaviorist era

The "Black Box" Problem

  • Memory and thought were dismissed as unscientific.
  • Mental processes could not be directly seen.
1948

Tolman's Cognitive Maps

  • Demonstrated "cognitive maps" in rats.
  • Proved that internal mental representations exist.
Key Message

Behaviourism pushed the mind out of science, until Tolman showed that internal representations exist.

Slide 4 • Timeline 2 of 3

The Cognitive Revolution (1950s–1960s)

1950s – 1960s

Replacing behaviourism with the information-processing model

1950s–1960s

The Computer Analogy

  • Replaced behaviourism with the Information Processing Model.
  • The mind is modelled as Input → Storage / Processing → Output.
1956

George Miller

  • Proved human capacity limits with the "Magical Number 7 ± 2".
  • Working memory has a limited capacity.
1959

Noam Chomsky

  • Refuted Skinner, proving language requires innate cognitive rules.
  • Language is not just learned through conditioning.
1956

MIT Symposium

  • Marked the formal birth of cognitive science.
  • Gave the field a formal starting point.
Key Message

The computer analogy brought internal processes back into science, and language showed the mind is more than conditioning.

Slide 5 • Timeline 3 of 3

Formalizing & Modern Cognitive Neuroscience

1967 – Today

From a named field to brain science and artificial intelligence

1967

Ulric Neisser

  • Published the foundational textbook Cognitive Psychology, coining the term.
  • Defined cognition as how sensory input is transformed, reduced, elaborated, stored, recovered, and used.
1968

Structural Models

  • Formalization of the Atkinson-Shiffrin Memory Model.
  • Early theories of selective attention emerged.
1970s–1980s

Brain Imaging

  • Merged cognitive theories with EEG, PET, and fMRI.
  • Moved from theoretical models to identifying the brain structures responsible for thought.
Today

Current Horizons

  • Expansion into Artificial Intelligence and computational neural networks (connectionism).
  • Embodied cognition.
Key Message

Neisser named the field, and brain imaging then began linking its theories to actual brain structures.

Myth or Fact

Statement #3

"Blind people experience a drastic, superhuman sharpening of their physical sense of hearing."

IT'S A MYTH!

The Scientific Fact:

Their physical hearing mechanics are identical to sighted people; instead, the brain repurposes the unused visual cortex to process auditory information, allowing for superior cognitive processing of echoes and sound locations.

Case Study: Elizabeth F. Loftus

Name

Elizabeth F. Loftus

Profession

Cognitive Psychologist and Researcher

Specialization

Human Memory and Eyewitness Testimony

Studied how people form, store, and recall memories.

Known for her research on the Misinformation Effect.

Slide 2 of 3 • Career Path

Education and Career Journey

1966 • UCLA

Completed her Bachelor's degree in Mathematics and Psychology from UCLA in 1966.

Stanford University

Earned her Master's degree and PhD from Stanford University.

Researcher and Professor

Worked as a psychology researcher and university professor.

Academic Positions

Held academic positions at the University of Washington and the University of California, Irvine.

Dedicated her research to understanding human memory and its reliability.

Slide 3 of 3 • Research

Major Research and Contributions

Studied how misleading information can change people's memories.

Demonstrated that the wording of questions can influence eyewitness recollections.

Conducted influential research with John Palmer in 1974 on memory and car accident descriptions.

Her research contributed to the understanding of eyewitness testimony in legal investigations.

Conclusion

Elizabeth Loftus's work demonstrates how cognitive psychology helps us understand memory and its role in everyday life and the justice system.
